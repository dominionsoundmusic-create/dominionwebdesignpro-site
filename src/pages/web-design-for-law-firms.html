What good web design for law firms looks like

Good web design for law firms starts from the visitor's state of mind. Someone looking for a lawyer is rarely relaxed. They were just served papers, or arrested, or hurt, or they are watching a parent's health decline and wondering about a will. They type a plain question into a phone, and the firm whose page answers that question clearly, in plain English, gets the call.

A law firm site also has rules that a plumber's site does not. Lawyer advertising is regulated by each state, the American Bar Association's Model Rules shape most of those state rules, and a careless contact form can create obligations you never meant to take on. We build with those rules in mind, and we leave the final word on compliance to you and your ethics counsel, because we are web designers, not lawyers.

Practice-area pages do the heavy lifting

A single "Practice Areas" page with eight bullet points tells a visitor what you do. It does not help them find you. People search for the problem they have: "what happens at a first DWI hearing," "do I need a lawyer to probate a small estate," "how long does a divorce take in my county." A page for each practice area, and sometimes for each common question within it, gives each of those searches somewhere to land.

Each practice-area page we write explains, in general terms, how that kind of matter usually works, what a person should gather before a consultation, and how to reach you. It does not promise outcomes, and it carries a short note that general information is not legal advice.

Lawyer advertising rules a website has to live with

This is the background we write against. It is not legal advice, and your state's current rules and your own counsel control.

ABA Model Rule 7.1

The Model Rule says a lawyer shall not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer or the lawyer's services. A statement can be misleading even if it is literally true, for example when it omits a fact needed to make the whole statement not misleading, or when it is likely to create unjustified expectations. Most states have adopted a version of it, sometimes with additions, so your state's text is the one that counts.

Texas: content rules and filing

In Texas, the Disciplinary Rules of Professional Conduct govern lawyer advertising. Rules 7.01 through 7.03 cover the content of communications, and Rule 7.04 requires certain advertisements to be filed with the State Bar's Advertising Review Committee no later than ten days after they are first disseminated. Rule 7.05(b) exempts information posted on a law firm website except the homepage contents, unless another exemption applies. The whole website still has to follow the content rules.

What that means for how we write: no superlatives we cannot back up, no comparisons with other lawyers, no promised results, and no reviews or case outcomes we made up. If your rules require a specific disclosure, a responsible-attorney name or a filing, tell Maurice and it goes on the site the way your counsel words it.

The intake form problem, and how we handle it

The contact form is where a law firm website can create real trouble. The ABA's Formal Opinion 10-457 on lawyer websites explains that a site which simply lists contact information generally does not start a prospective-client relationship, but a site that specifically invites people to submit information about a possible matter can. Once that happens, the firm may owe duties to that person, and what they send could even affect whether the firm can represent someone on the other side.

So the forms we build for firms are deliberately short:

  • Name, phone, email and the best time to call
  • A general topic chosen from a list (for example family law, estate planning, criminal defense), not a free-text "tell us everything" box
  • A clear notice right beside the form, worded by you or your counsel, saying that submitting it does not create an attorney-client relationship and asking people not to send confidential details yet
  • A link to your privacy policy
  • Leads emailed straight to the inbox you choose

The same caution applies to a chat widget. If you add the AI chat on the Pro package, it is set up to collect a name and number and point people to call, not to take down the facts of their case.

Attorney bios that read like a person

After the practice-area page, the attorney bio is often the most visited page on a small firm's site. People want to know who they will actually sit across from. A good bio says where you practice, which courts you appear in, what kinds of matters you handle, bar admissions, education, and a little about why you do this work.

What it leaves out: claims of specialization your state does not allow you to make, invented honors, and anything you cannot document. The ABA Model Rules limit when a lawyer may say they are a specialist, and states set their own versions of that limit. If you hold a recognized specialty credential, you tell us exactly how the issuing body says to describe it.

Location pages for the courthouses you work in

People often search with a place in mind: "divorce lawyer in Smith County," "probate attorney Tyler," "criminal defense near Longview." A firm that appears in several courthouses can give each of those places its own page, written about how matters tend to move there and who the firm serves in that area, not the same paragraph with the county name changed.

Those pages have to follow the same advertising rules as the rest of the site. They should not suggest an office where you have none, and they should not imply results. If you meet clients only at your main office or by video, the page says so. Pro includes ten researched city pages; on Agency, keyword research decides which location and practice pairings are worth writing at all, so you are not paying for pages nobody searches for.
