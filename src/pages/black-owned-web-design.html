For most small business owners hiring a web designer, the first row is the one that matters, as long as it is honest and specific. For a buyer at a company or agency with a supplier diversity goal, only the formal certifications usually count.

What MBE certification is, and what it is not

The National Minority Supplier Development Council (NMSDC) runs one of the best-known programs. Its definition of a minority business enterprise is a for-profit business that is at least 51% owned, operated and controlled by minority group members, and NMSDC lists Asian-Indian, Asian-Pacific, Black, Hispanic and Native American individuals as the groups it recognizes. The owners must be U.S. citizens and must be active in running the company day to day, not owners on paper only. The council checks this through documents, interviews and a site visit before it lists a firm.

That review is why large buyers rely on it. A corporation tracking how much it spends with minority suppliers needs more than a self-reported badge. NMSDC is also clear that certification opens doors to buyers; it does not promise any contracts.

Owner-claimed and formally reviewed are two different statements, and an honest company keeps them apart. Dominion Web Design Pro is Black-owned. It does not hold an MBE certification, an NMSDC listing or any state or city minority business certification, and nothing on this site says otherwise. If your purchasing department needs a certification number for its records, ask for it in writing from any vendor and look the number up with the council that issued it.

Why spending with Black-owned businesses still matters

Black-owned companies are a real and growing part of the economy, and they are still underrepresented among businesses large enough to hire staff. The U.S. Census Bureau's Annual Business Survey counted about 201,000 Black or African American-owned employer businesses in reference year 2023, with $249.0 billion in receipts. That was 3.4% of the roughly 5.9 million employer firms in the country, while the Census Bureau's population estimates put people who identify as Black alone at about 13.5% of Americans.

Most Black-owned businesses are one-person operations. The same Census release counted about 4.4 million Black or African American-owned nonemployer businesses in 2023, firms with no paid employees, with $128.7 billion in receipts. A lot of growing companies start right there: one owner, one truck or one chair, and a phone that needs to ring more often.

Access to money is part of why the gap persists. The Federal Reserve Banks' 2021 Small Business Credit Survey report on firms owned by people of color, based on a survey fielded in the fall of 2020, found that in the prior 12 months 13% of Black-owned firms that applied for non-emergency financing received all of it, compared with 40% of white-owned firms. When outside credit is harder to get, a business has to grow on the customers it can win, which is exactly the job a website is supposed to do.

None of that makes a site better or worse because of who built it. It is a reason some owners choose where their money goes, and that is their call to make.

Questions to ask any web developer before you sign

These apply to every Black owned web design agency, every freelancer and every large firm, including us. A good developer will answer all of them without hesitating.

  • What is the total price, in dollars, in writing? Including what renews monthly or yearly, and what happens if you stop paying it.
  • Who will register the domain, and in whose name? ICANN's registrant rules put the registered name holder in charge of the domain and its contact details. That should be you or your company, not the designer.
  • Who writes the pages? Hand-written for your business, or copied from a template and swapped for your name.
  • Can I see my site before I pay in full? A demo, a mockup or at least a draft you can open on your phone.
  • Who will I talk to after launch? A named person, or a ticket queue.
  • What will you not promise? Anyone who promises a ranking position or a set number of leads is guessing. Nobody controls Google.
  • If you claim a certification, which body issued it? Then look it up with that body.
