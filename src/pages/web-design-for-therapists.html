What web design for therapists has to do differently

Web design for therapists is less about selling and more about lowering a barrier. Most people reading a counselor's website have thought about reaching out for weeks or months. They are reading closely, often late at night, trying to answer one question: would this person understand me? A site that answers that in your own voice, explains the practical details plainly and makes the first step small will do more than any amount of design polish.

It also has to be careful in ways a contractor's site does not. The contact form should not invite someone to type out their history. The site should say clearly that it is not a crisis line. And any statement about privacy should be accurate, not a reassuring badge.

Dominion Web Design Pro builds private practice websites one at a time. Maurice Johnson writes every page by hand from what you tell him about your work, and you see a free demo before you commit to anything.

Your own site or a directory profile?

Many therapists get their first referrals from directories such as Psychology Today, where professionals pay a fixed monthly fee to be listed. That is a reasonable place to be found. It is not a replacement for a site of your own, and the two do different jobs.

The usual answer is both: keep the directory profile and link it to your own site, where the visitor can read more and request a consultation.

Specialty pages, written in your voice

"Anxiety, depression, trauma, relationships, life transitions" on one page reads like every other practice in town. A page for each area you genuinely focus on, written the way you would explain it in a first session, gives the right reader a reason to stay, and gives searches like "therapist for postpartum anxiety" or "couples counseling" a page to land on.

Maurice works from a conversation or a questionnaire with you, not from a template. You read every page before it goes live and change anything that does not sound like you. Nothing claims an outcome, and no client stories are invented.

Contact forms that do not collect health details

HIPAA applies to covered entities. HHS explains that a health care provider is a covered entity if it transmits health information electronically in connection with a transaction HHS has adopted a standard for, such as submitting insurance claims. Plenty of private-pay therapists fall outside that, and plenty of in-network ones fall inside it. We are not the right people to tell you which you are or what your obligations are; your compliance advisor or attorney is.

What we can do is keep the website out of the clinical side entirely. Every therapist site we build follows these rules:

  • The contact form asks for a name, a way to reach the person, and a preferred time. No reason for seeking therapy, no symptoms, no history.
  • A short line beside the form asks people not to include personal health information and to save that for the first conversation.
  • Intake paperwork, scheduling and notes stay in whatever practice management system you already use. The website links to its portal if you have one.
  • Advertising pixels and session-recording tools are left off unless you and your advisor decide otherwise.
  • The privacy policy describes what the site actually collects, in plain words.

The same goes for the AI chat widget on the Pro package. For a practice, it is set up to answer general questions about fees, hours and how to book, and to take a name and number, never to discuss anyone's situation. Many therapists will reasonably decide to skip it.

A crisis note on every page

Your website is not watched around the clock, and someone in crisis may land on it. Every therapist site we build carries a short, visible note in the footer and on the contact page: if you are in crisis, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, which offers free, confidential support by call, text and chat around the clock, or call 911 in an emergency.

The wording is yours to approve. If your state or your licensing board expects other language, we use theirs.

What a private practice site usually needs

  1. HomeWho you work with, how, where (in person, telehealth, or both), and one clear way to request a first call.
  2. AboutYour training, your license and the states it covers, and why you do this work, in your words.
  3. Specialty pagesOne for each area you focus on.
  4. Fees and insuranceYour session fee, whether you are in network with any plans, superbills, sliding scale if you offer one and your cancellation policy.
  5. ContactThe short form, your phone line, office location if you have one, and the crisis note.
