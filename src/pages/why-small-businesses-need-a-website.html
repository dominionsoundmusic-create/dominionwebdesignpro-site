Why does a small business need a website? Because before most people call a business they have not used, they look it up, and the website is the only place online where you decide what they see. Your Google listing, your Facebook page and the review sites all belong to someone else and follow their rules. A website is yours: your services, your prices or price ranges, your service area, your photos and your phone number, laid out the way you would explain them in person.

That said, this is not a page that pretends every business on earth needs a website tomorrow. Some do not yet. Below are the real survey numbers (including where they disagree), the situations where you can wait, and how a website fits next to a Google Business Profile and a Facebook page.

Small business website statistics, from named surveys

There is no official government count of how many small businesses have a website, so every number comes from a private survey. Here are the ones we could verify, with who ran them and when.

  • 83% have a websiteClutch surveyed 406 U.S. small business owners on August 5, 2025. Its press release compares that with 64% in 2018; another Clutch article on the same survey says 65%, so the 2018 baseline is a point or so fuzzy.
  • 17% are still offlineThe other side of the same Clutch survey.
  • 34% of those see no needAmong owners without a site, the top reason was that a website did not seem relevant to their industry.
  • 16% blame the costDown from 26% in Clutch's 2018 survey. Most owners without a site cite something other than money.

Other roundups repeat older figures, often without saying who was asked or how, so we left them out. The honest summary is that about four in five small businesses now have a site, and Clutch's own numbers show the share rising since 2018.

What customers do before they call you

The case for a website is less about statistics on businesses and more about what customers do.

They read reviews

BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey of 1,002 U.S. adults found 97% read online reviews, and 41% always read them when looking at local businesses, up from 29% the year before.

Then they look for details

Reviews say whether people liked you. They rarely say what you charge, which towns you cover, whether you handle their exact problem, or how soon you can come. That is the website's job.

Then they compare

A searcher with a leaking roof is looking at two or three businesses at once. The one with a clear page about roof leaks in their town has an easier time getting the call than the one with only a phone number.

When your small business does not need a website yet

Some owners are better off putting the money somewhere else for now. You can reasonably wait if:

  • You are booked solid from referrals and repeat work. A plumber with a six-week waitlist from word of mouth does not have a customer-finding problem this month.
  • You are still testing the idea. Before you know what you sell and to whom, a Google Business Profile and a simple social page are enough to learn from.
  • You work only through someone else's platform. A booth vendor who sells through a market's own site, or a contractor who works only as a subcontractor, may not need public pages.
  • The money is needed for tools or payroll. A website should pay for itself. If it would crowd out what keeps the doors open, wait, and set up the free Google profile in the meantime.

What usually ends the waiting: wanting to raise prices, adding a service, moving into a new town, hiring, or noticing a competitor with a clear website getting jobs you used to get. Any of those means people now need to learn about you from someone other than a friend.

Google Business Profile or a website? You want both

A Google Business Profile is free and it is what shows up on Google Maps and in the map results. If you have neither a profile nor a website, set up the profile first, today. It takes an afternoon.

But the profile is not a website, and Google made that clear in 2024. It used to let owners build a basic website inside the Business Profile, with addresses ending in business.site. Google turned those sites off in early March 2024 and sent visitors to the owner's profile only until June 10, 2024. After that, the links went nowhere. Owners who had relied on them had to build a real site and update the address on every listing that pointed to the old one.

The lesson is not that Google is unreliable. It is that anything you build on someone else's platform can change under you. The profile gets you found; the website, on a domain in your name, is what you own. The U.S. Small Business Administration puts it plainly: once you register a domain name, no one else can use it for as long as you keep it.

The Facebook-only business

Plenty of good small businesses run entirely on a Facebook page, and for some of them it works. A food truck posting today's location, a bakery posting the weekly menu: Facebook is fast and their regulars are already there.

The trouble starts when growth depends on strangers. Google results for a service in your town are mostly websites and map listings, not Facebook posts. Some people will not log in to Facebook to read a page. Posts scroll away, so the details a new customer needs, like your service area, your hours and what you charge, end up buried under last month's photos. And the page itself is rented: if the account is locked or the rules change, years of posts can become hard to reach.

The fix is not to quit Facebook. Keep posting there, and point every post and the page's link field to a website that holds the permanent answers.

Small business website tips: what the site has to do

A useful small business website does a few plain things well. Fancy features come a distant second.

  1. Say what you do and where, at the top."Roof repair in Tyler and Longview" beats a slogan. A visitor should know in three seconds whether they are in the right place.
  2. Put the phone number where a thumb can tap it.Plenty of local searchers are on a phone, and they want to call, not scroll.
  3. Give each main service its own page.Searchers look for the specific thing they need. One page that lists everything matches none of it well.
  4. Show the towns you serve.A service area page, or a page for each main town, tells Google and the customer that you come to them.
  5. Answer the questions people ask on the phone.Price ranges, how long jobs take, whether you are licensed where your trade requires it. Every answer on the site is one less reason to call someone else.
  6. Keep it current.Old hours and dead links tell a visitor nobody is minding the shop.

Six questions to settle it for your own business

Skip the general arguments and answer these about your own shop. Two or more yes answers usually mean the website should move up the list.

  1. In the last month, did anyone tell you they almost called someone else because they could not find your hours, prices or service area?
  2. Do you want work from people who have never heard of you, not just from people who already know you?
  3. Does a competitor with a clear website show up when you search for your own service and town?
  4. Do you spend phone time answering the same five questions that a page could answer?
  5. Are you adding a service, a town or an employee in the next year?
  6. Would you be embarrassed to send a new customer to your current online presence?

If every answer is no, keep the money for now and revisit this in six months. If several are yes, the website is not a luxury. It is a salesperson that works the night shift.
