What contractor website design should actually do

Good contractor website design has one job: when a homeowner finds you, it gives them enough reason to pick up the phone instead of the next result. A person with water in the kitchen or a half-finished deck does not compare twelve contractors at a desk. They search, skim a few sites on a phone in a few seconds, and call whoever looks real, nearby and able to do the job. Most of the deciding is done before your phone rings.

That is why we build contractor sites around three things a homeowner looks for: photos of work that is plainly yours, a clear list of the jobs you take, and a phone number they can tap without hunting. Everything else is secondary.

Dominion Web Design Pro builds these sites for general contractors, remodelers, builders and tradesmen. Maurice Johnson writes every page by hand for your business and the towns you cover, and you see a working demo before any money changes hands.

What a website for a contractor needs, and what it can skip

Not a lot, and less than most agencies will try to sell you. Here is the short list we build every contractor site from. If a page or feature is not on it, it has to earn its place.

Photos of your own jobs

Not stock and not renderings. Pictures from your phone, ideally before and after, taken before you pack up the tools. Real job photos are the biggest single difference between a site that reads as an actual company and one that reads as a template. They cost nothing but the habit.

A page for each service you sell

One page that lists eight services tends to rank for none of them. Separate pages for kitchen remodels, bathroom remodels and room additions each have a chance at their own searches, because that is how homeowners type.

A number that works with one tap

Most of your visitors are holding a phone, often standing in the yard looking at the problem. The number should be a tap-to-call link at the top of every page, never an image and never text they have to copy.

Some honest word about price

You do not need a rate card. A starting figure, a typical range or a plain explanation of what moves the number is enough. People reading about price have already decided they want the work done, and those are good leads to keep.

The towns you actually drive to

A service area section on every page, and a dedicated page for each town worth targeting. Not two hundred towns you would never drive to: the ones you want work in.

Speed on a phone

A page that takes several seconds to show anything loses people standing in a driveway. We compress photos, serve them in modern formats at the right size, and keep the code lean.

What you can usually skip: animated sliders, a blog nobody will write, stock photos of smiling models in hard hats, and a "portfolio" page with three pictures in it. A short gallery of real work on each service page does more.

Why we will not sell you two hundred city pages

A common pitch in web design for tradesmen goes like this: a page for every service in every town in the metro, generated automatically, three hundred or more chances to rank. It is cheap to produce, which is exactly why it is offered.

The trouble is that Google has no obligation to index pages that only swap a town name. Many of those pages end up in Search Console listed as "Discovered, currently not indexed," which Google's own help describes as a page it has found but not yet crawled. Pages can stay in that state for a long time. Two pages that share most of their wording give Google no reason to spend time on either one.

So we work the other way. Every town page is researched first: the town's business makeup, its main roads, why someone there would search the way they do. Then it is written from scratch. It is slower, and it is why the Pro package has ten city pages and Agency usually lands between thirty and sixty instead of three hundred. If someone offers you a contractor website with two hundred city pages included, ask them how many are indexed on the last site they built. You can read more on how city pages work.

The three things most contractors are missing

  1. Reviews, asked for at the right momentFor anything typed with "near me," the map results sit near the top, and reviews help decide who gets the call. BrightLocal's 2025 consumer survey found only 4% of people say they never read online reviews. Asking is free. The best time is when the job is done and the customer is standing in front of it, pleased.
  2. Somebody, or something, answering the phoneA homeowner who reaches voicemail often calls the next result instead of waiting. Whatever you do about the website, fixing missed calls is worth doing. If you are on a roof or under a house all day, that is what the AI phone agent on the Agency plan is for.
  3. Owning your own siteA lot of contractors are renting. The site lives on an agency's platform, the domain sits in someone else's account, and when the monthly payment stops it all disappears. Ask whoever built yours one question: if I stop paying you, what do I keep? With us the answer is the whole site.
