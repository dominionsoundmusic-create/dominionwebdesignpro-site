How do I find a web designer? Ask two or three business owners you trust who built their site, look at websites you like in your own trade and find out who made them, then talk to two or three designers and hire the one who shows you real work and answers every ownership question in writing. That is the short version. The rest of this guide is the detail: where to look, what to ask, what an RFP and a contract should say, how domain ownership really works, and the warning signs that send most owners back to square one.

We are a web designer, so read this knowing that. Everything below applies just as much to hiring us as to hiring anyone else.

Where to look for a web designer

Owners you already trust

A referral from a business owner with a similar budget is the best lead you can get. Ask what they paid in total, how long it took, and whether they can log in to their own site today. That last answer tells you a lot.

Sites you like in your trade

Find three websites from businesses like yours, in other towns, that make you want to call them. Many have a small credit line at the bottom naming who built them. If not, a polite email to the owner usually gets an answer.

Google Maps and local search

Search for web designers in your town on Google Maps. Read their sites the way a customer would read yours: is the price clear, is a person named, does the phone get answered or returned?

Chamber and small business centers

Your local chamber of commerce lists member businesses, and Small Business Development Centers and SCORE chapters offer free or low-cost advising and workshops where you can ask how other owners found their designers.

Freelance marketplaces and directory sites with reviews are another route. Treat review scores there as one signal among several, and always open the live sites in a designer's portfolio yourself rather than trusting screenshots. Ask whether the business on each one is a paying client or a sample. Both are fine, but you should know which is which.

How to hire a web designer: the questions to ask on the first call

Ask every candidate the same questions and write the answers down. Vague answers are an answer.

  1. Who writes the words?Many designers build the layout and expect you to supply every sentence. That is fine if you know it going in. It is a shock if you do not.
  2. What is the total cost for the first year?One-time fee, hosting, domain, email, plugins, maintenance. Get the whole number, not just the build price.
  3. Who owns the domain, the files and the content?The right answer is you, for all three, with the domain registered in your name or your company's name.
  4. Can I see live sites you built, and what do those owners pay now?Live, on a phone, not just screenshots.
  5. How many rounds of changes are included?And what happens to the price after that.
  6. How long, and what do you need from me?Most delays are waiting on the owner for photos and details. A good designer tells you exactly what to gather.
  7. Who do I call after launch?A name, a number and how fast they usually reply.

How to choose a web design company once you have quotes

Put the answers side by side. Quotes for a small business site can run from a few hundred dollars to many thousands, and the price alone does not tell you much. What tells you more is what each price includes. A $3,000 quote that includes written pages, local SEO setup and a year of hosting can be a better deal than a $900 quote that hands you an empty template and an invoice for writing later.

Reviews of a website design company are worth reading for one thing in particular: how the company behaved when something went wrong. Every project has a hiccup. You are looking for the replies where the designer fixed it.

Writing a web design RFP that gets useful answers

A request for proposal sounds like something only city governments do, but a short one helps any business compare designers fairly. It forces you to decide what you want before anyone pitches you, and it gets every designer answering the same questions. For a local business, two to four pages is enough. Cover these:

  • About the businessWhat you do, who your customers are, the towns you serve, and your current site if there is one.
  • The job of the sitePhone calls, quote requests, bookings, walk-ins. Name the one that matters most.
  • Pages you expectHome, each main service, about, service area, contact, plus any city pages.
  • Words and photosSay whether you will supply them or want the designer to write and source them.
  • Budget rangeA real range saves everyone time. Designers scope to the money.
  • DeadlineWhen you need it live, and why, if there is a reason.
  • OwnershipState that the domain, content and design files belong to your business.
  • Hosting and supportAsk what it costs per month after launch and what it covers.
  • How you will decidePrice, examples, response time, ownership terms. Give a reply-by date.

The flip side is the web design questionnaire a designer sends you. If a designer quotes without asking about your customers, your towns and your competitors, the quote is a guess. You can see the questions we ask on our intake form; borrow them for your RFP if they help.

Domain ownership: the one thing to get right

The most painful web design stories are not about ugly sites. They are about owners who cannot get their own domain back after a designer disappears or a relationship sours.

Here is how it works. A .com or similar domain is registered through a registrar, a company accredited by ICANN, the nonprofit that coordinates the internet's domain name system. The person or company named as the registrant is the one who holds the registration. ICANN's guidance for registrants says the registrant has the right to move the domain to a different registrar, and that the transfer is requested through the registrar you are moving to. A registrar can refuse a transfer only for reasons listed in ICANN's Transfer Policy, such as a court order, a pending domain dispute or evidence of fraud, and transfers can be locked for a period after a new registration or a change of registrant.

So the registrant name on your domain matters more than anything a designer tells you. Ask for the domain to be registered in your business's name, with your email as the contact, and with login access you control. If a designer needs to work on it, add them as a user or share access, and change the password when the work is done.

What the contract should cover

A one-page agreement is fine for a small project, as long as it answers these in plain words:

  • The pages being built, by name, and who writes them.
  • The total price, the payment schedule, and every monthly fee after launch.
  • How many rounds of changes are included, and the rate after that.
  • The launch date, and what you must deliver for the designer to hit it.
  • That the domain, the content and the finished design belong to you once paid.
  • What hosting includes, and how you move the site if you leave.
  • How either side can end the agreement, and what you get if it ends midway.

Red flags that send owners back to square one

  • The domain is in the designer's name. Even with good intentions, it ties your business to their availability.
  • Full payment before you see any work. A deposit is normal. All of it, sight unseen, is a risk you do not need to take.
  • Ranking promises. Nobody controls Google. A designer who promises page one or a number of leads is telling you what you want to hear.
  • No live examples. Mockups are fine for a new designer, but you should still see something that works on a phone.
  • A long required monthly plan to keep the site you paid for. Ask what happens to the site if you stop paying.
  • Nobody answers. If it is hard to reach them before they have your money, it will not get easier after.

If you only remember three things

Get the domain in your own name. Get every cost, including the monthly ones, in writing. And judge the designer by real work you can open on your phone, ideally work made for your own business.

Everything else, from colors to fonts, can be fixed later. Those three are hard to fix once the check has cleared.
