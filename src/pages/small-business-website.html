Three ways a small business website can be laid out

People ask for small business website examples so they can picture their own. These are structure patterns, not client sites: three common kinds of local business and the shape of the website that fits each one.

The service business that drives to you

A plumber, pest control company or mobile detailer. Customers never visit the shop, so the address matters less than the service radius. The home page leads with the phone number and the towns covered. Each main service gets its own page because people search "water heater repair" and "drain cleaning" separately.

The shop customers walk into

A boutique, a cafe, a salon or a hardware store. Hours, parking and the map go near the top. Photos of the inside of the place do more than any paragraph. The services page may be a menu, a price list or a short list of brands carried.

The office people book

An accountant, insurance agent, counselor or tutor. Trust carries the sale, so the about page gets real attention: who you are, what you will and will not take on, and what the first appointment looks like. The contact page explains how to book and what to bring.

Common problems we see on small business websites

Most small business sites do not fail for lack of design. They fail on a handful of plain things that cost calls every week.

  • The phone number is hard to tap. On a phone, the number should be a button near the top of every page, not an image of a number and not hidden in the footer.
  • Nobody can tell where you work. A site that never names its towns leaves search engines and customers guessing.
  • One page tries to say everything. A single long page with every service on it is hard to read and hard for search to match to a specific job.
  • Words written for the owner, not the customer. Mission statements and industry terms read like filler. Customers want to know whether you do their job, when you can come and roughly what happens next.
  • The owner cannot get in. The domain, the hosting or the login belongs to a vendor who stopped answering. That is the most expensive problem on the list, and it is avoidable.
  • The form goes nowhere. A contact form that sends messages to an old inbox looks fine and quietly loses every lead.

Web design for startups and brand new businesses

A startup has a particular problem: no reviews yet, no gallery of past work, maybe not even a sign on the door. The temptation is to fill the gap with stock photos of smiling strangers and a few borrowed quotes. Do not do it. Customers can tell, and fake reviews create trouble you do not need.

What a new business site should do instead is be specific. Name the services. Name the towns. Say who you are and why you started. Put up your own photos, even phone photos of your truck, your tools or your counter. Add the reviews and the gallery as they come in.

Before any of that, register the domain in the business's own name. The U.S. Small Business Administration says that if you want an online presence, start by registering a domain name, and it advises buying web design, hosting and domain names in the company's name rather than the owner's. That is good advice for an established shop too.
