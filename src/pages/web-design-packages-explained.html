What a web design package is

Web design packages are fixed bundles a web designer or agency sells at set prices, usually in three tiers. Each tier promises a number of pages and a list of features: a contact form, mobile layout, some search setup, maybe a blog, hosting or a few months of support. Packages exist because they make a custom service easy to buy. You pick a tier instead of waiting on a custom proposal.

That convenience has a cost. A package list is written to sell, and two packages with nearly the same bullet points can be very different jobs. The trick is to read past the headline numbers and find out what work is actually being done, by whom, and what you will pay every month afterward.

The building blocks inside most packages

Strip away the tier names and nearly every package is made from the same seven parts. The price differences come from how much of each part is included.

  • PagesA number, sometimes with a list: home, services, about, contact. Higher tiers add service pages, location pages or a blog.
  • WordsEither you supply them, the designer edits yours, or the designer writes them. This single line often explains half the price gap between two quotes.
  • DesignA ready-made theme with your logo and colors, or a layout made for your business. Lower tiers are usually the first.
  • Search setupPage titles, descriptions, a sitemap, schema markup, sometimes a Google Business Profile. Some packages call this "SEO" and stop there.
  • HostingIncluded for a period, included for as long as you pay a monthly fee, or not included at all.
  • SupportFixes and changes after launch, either for a set number of weeks or through a monthly plan.
  • Add-onsBooking, online payments, live chat, AI tools, extra pages, logo work. Often priced separately even when the package mentions them.

Why almost every provider sells three tiers

Look at enough pricing pages and you will see the same shape: a cheap tier, a middle tier marked "most popular", and an expensive tier. That shape is partly about the work and partly about selling. The bottom tier sets a low starting price for the ads. The top tier makes the middle look reasonable. Most buyers pick the middle, which is often the tier the provider most wants to sell.

None of that makes tiers dishonest. Different businesses genuinely need different amounts of work: a one-location barber shop and a roofer covering forty towns are not buying the same website. The useful question is not which tier sounds best but which tier matches how your customers search. If people find you by searching your trade plus a town name, and you serve many towns, the extra pages in a higher tier may be the whole point. If nearly every customer comes from one town and word of mouth, the lowest tier that covers the basics properly is usually enough.

Before you choose, write down the three or four searches a new customer would type to find a business like yours. Then check which tier actually produces pages for those searches.

Web design and SEO packages: what the SEO part usually means

"Web design and SEO packages" can mean two very different things. The first is technical setup at launch: each page gets a unique title and description, the site has a sitemap submitted to Google, the business details are marked up with schema, and the Google Business Profile is connected. That is real work, it should be in any decent package, and it is a one-time job.

The second is ongoing SEO sold as a monthly line: content, links, reports. That can be useful, but only if the provider tells you what will be done each month in plain words. A monthly fee that buys a PDF report and nothing else is a subscription, not a service.

Whatever the package says, nobody controls Google's results. Google's own page on hiring an SEO warns against anyone who claims a special relationship with Google or promises first place in search results, and tells readers to find someone else if they hear it. Treat any package that sells a ranking the same way.

Six red flags in small business website design packages

  1. Page counts as the main selling point.A few hundred location pages made from one template with the town name swapped in rarely get read. Ask how each page is written.
  2. A promised ranking, date or number of leads.No one can promise where Google will place a page or how many people will call. Packages that put numbers like these in writing are guessing with your money.
  3. You do not own the domain.If the domain is registered to the provider, leaving them can mean losing the address your customers know. It should be in your name.
  4. The site only exists while you pay.Some packages are really rentals: a low monthly fee, and the site disappears when you stop. That can suit some businesses, but know it going in.
  5. No year two price.Introductory hosting and "first year free" support can double or triple at renewal. Get the year two total in writing.
  6. Vague words like "custom" and "premium".Ask what makes the design custom and what makes the tier premium. If the answer is a longer bullet list of the same features, the tiers are mostly pricing.
