Two customers, one address

The visitor on event day

Arlington's entertainment district holds AT&T Stadium, Globe Life Field, Six Flags Over Texas and Texas Live!. Those venues draw visitors from well beyond the city, and most of them do not know the streets. They search on a phone for food, a place to wait out traffic or a fix for a flat, and they choose fast.

The neighbor all year

The 2020 census counted 394,266 residents, making Arlington the second largest city in Tarrant County. UT Arlington adds tens of thousands of students. These are the customers who come back, leave reviews and tell their friends, and they want to know if you are close, what you charge and when you are open.

Most small businesses do not need two websites for this. They need one site with a clear home page, real hours and a service or menu page written in plain words, plus a short note for event days when something changes.

Web design for Arlington, TX customers who mostly drive

Arlington has long been known as a large city without fixed-route buses. In their place, the city runs an on-demand rideshare service with Via: riders book by app or phone and get picked up within a couple of blocks, and the service has added links to regional rail at the CentrePort TRE station. For most customers, though, getting to you means a car, a freeway and a parking spot.

That changes what a site should say. Name the nearest highway exit or big cross street, like Cooper Street at I-20 by The Parks Mall, mention parking, and keep the tap-to-call number at the top of every page. Someone coming from Grand Prairie or Mansfield wants to know the trip is worth it before they leave.

It matters for search as well. Google says distance is one of the three main things behind its local results, along with relevance and prominence, so a page that says exactly where in Arlington you are gives it something solid to work with.

A short game-day checklist for your site

If big events touch your business at all, a few small things on the website prevent a lot of missed sales and annoyed phone calls:

  • Event-day hours, written as text on the home page, not buried in a social media post.
  • A line on whether you are inside the event traffic area, and the best way in.
  • Your menu, price list or services as real text that loads quickly on a weak signal.
  • One button to call and one to get directions, both visible without scrolling.
  • A plain note when you are closed for an event, so nobody drives over for nothing.
