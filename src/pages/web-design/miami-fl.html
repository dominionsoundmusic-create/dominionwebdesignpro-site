Web design in Miami has a question most cities never ask: which language does the customer read first? In Miami-Dade County the honest answer for most households is not English. A bakery in Little Havana, a tax preparer in Doral and a hair salon near NE 2nd Avenue in Little Haiti all serve people who may search in Spanish, read the menu in English and call the shop speaking Creole. A good Miami website is built around that, not patched for it after launch.

Dominion Web Design Pro is Maurice Johnson, working from Big Sandy in East Texas. He writes each page by hand, shows you a free demo before any money changes hands, and charges one printed price. What follows is how we would plan a site for a Miami business, and where we are honest about our limits.

The numbers behind bilingual web design in Miami

The U.S. Census Bureau's QuickFacts table for Miami-Dade County puts the Hispanic or Latino share of residents at 70.6%. It also reports that 75.3% of residents age 5 and older speak a language other than English at home, and that 54.5% were born outside the United States. Those are county figures, so a single block may look very different, but they explain why a Miami owner cannot treat Spanish as an afterthought.

{{ img("demo-images/salon/4.jpg", "Row of empty leather styling chairs in a bright salon with warm lamps and shelves of products", 1344, 768) }}

Language shows up in small places on a site. A salon booking form that only says "Submit" in English loses the client who would have booked in Spanish. A clinic that lists services in one language and insurance notes in another confuses everyone. The fix is planning: decide which pages need two languages, keep the phone number and address identical on both, and make sure each version reads like a person wrote it.

Many owners in Miami already handle this at the counter every day without thinking about it. The website just needs to catch up with how the business actually talks.

Why a translate button is not enough

This site has an EN/ES button at the top. It runs on Google's machine translation, and it is useful for a visitor who wants the gist of a page. It is not how a Miami business should present itself in Spanish. Machine output misses the way a Cuban grandmother, a Venezuelan engineer and a Colombian contractor each expect to be addressed, and it can turn a friendly line into a stiff one.

So here is how Spanish pages work with us, plainly. Maurice writes the English pages from a conversation with you. The Spanish pages are written or reviewed by you, a family member or a fluent person you trust, and then we build them as real pages with their own titles and descriptions so Google can show them to Spanish searchers. Maurice does not claim to write fluent Spanish copy himself, and we would rather say that now than have you discover it in a draft.

  • Separate Spanish pages with their own page titles, not a widget layered over English.
  • The same phone number, address and hours in both versions.
  • A language link in the header so a visitor switches in one tap.
  • Your fluent reviewer signs off on every Spanish page before launch.

Four Miami neighborhoods, four different website jobs

Miami is not one market. The way people find a business changes from one neighborhood to the next, and a site should name the places it really serves.

Little Havana and Calle Ocho

A Little Havana community background report hosted by Florida International University notes that most neighborhood businesses are still Cuban-owned even as the area has grown more diverse. Calle Ocho is known for restaurants, walk-up coffee windows, family cigar shops, botanicas and Latin music stores. Viernes Culturales, a free street festival usually held on the third Friday of the month, brings crowds to Calle Ocho. A restaurant there needs a menu that loads as text in both languages and hours that are right on festival nights.

Little Haiti

NE 2nd Avenue is the neighborhood's main commercial strip, and the City of Miami formally designated Little Haiti in 2016. Haitian-owned shops, bookstores and restaurants line it. Here the third language matters: a short page in Haitian Creole, written by someone fluent, can be the difference for a family that reads Creole first.

Wynwood

The Wynwood Business Improvement District describes the area as galleries, retail, antique shops, bars and restaurants around one of the largest open-air street art installations in the world. Before the art, it was a garment district. Businesses here sell to visitors who found them on a phone five minutes ago, so photos, a map and a fast mobile page carry the weight.

Allapattah

Along NW 17th Avenue, the stretch nicknamed Little Santo Domingo reflects decades of Dominican families and businesses. Around NW 20th Street the neighborhood also has a long-running garment and fabric wholesale trade, alongside produce wholesalers, bodegas and small shops. Wholesale buyers and everyday customers want a phone number, a list of what you carry and directions, without a lot of reading.

Beyond the city line, places like Doral, Hialeah and Coral Gables are separate cities with their own customers searching by name. That is exactly what city pages are for. The Pro package includes ten of them, and each is written about that place rather than copied with the name swapped.

Local search in Miami-Dade is a two-language contest

Google says local results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence. Relevance is the part your website controls: a page that clearly says what you do, in which neighborhood and in the language the searcher used gives Google something to match. A Spanish searcher typing "dentista cerca de mí" is far more likely to find a practice that has a real Spanish page than one that leans on a translate widget.

Nobody can promise you a position in the results, and we will not. What we can do is make sure each version of your site says the same true things about your business, with your service area named, your phone number tappable and your Google Business Profile set up on the Pro package.
