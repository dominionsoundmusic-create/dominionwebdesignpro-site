Web design in Conyers, GA serves a county seat that grew up beside a railroad and grew again beside an interstate. Conyers began as a stop on the Georgia Railroad in the 1840s, took its name from Dr. W. D. Conyers, a Covington banker who bought the land and deeded the right of way, and was incorporated in 1854. When Rockdale County was carved out of Newton County in 1870, Conyers became its seat. The 2020 census counted 17,305 people in the city, about 24 miles east of downtown Atlanta, and Interstate 20, built through the county in the 1960s, still carries much of the traffic that local businesses depend on.

Olde Town Conyers and the courthouse trade

The city's original commercial blocks are now called Olde Town Conyers, and the New Georgia Encyclopedia describes them as home to attorneys and specialty shops. The city's Downtown Development Authority was created to revitalize and redevelop that district, and it uses the Main Street approach for the historic downtown. The Main Street program works out of the Conyers Welcome Center in the historic depot at 901 Railroad Street.

For a shop or law office there, a website has two audiences. Locals already know the street and want hours, parking and a phone number. Visitors who drove in for an event want to know whether the place is worth a stop. A short page that answers both, with honest photos of the inside of the shop, will outperform a long one full of slogans.

The Horse Park brings visitors who search on their phones

The Georgia International Horse Park sits on more than 1,000 acres that the city first acquired for wastewater use. It hosted the equestrian events, the mountain bike race and the final two modern pentathlon events of the 1996 Atlanta Olympics, and it still holds horse shows, fairs, festivals and other events through the year. The Conyers Cherry Blossom Festival, which began in 1982, moved there in 1997. South of I-20, the Monastery of the Holy Spirit, home to Cistercian monks, runs a heritage center with a visitors center and abbey store that draws its own guests.

Every one of those visitors is a potential customer for a restaurant, a hotel, a feed store, a tire shop or a gas station nearby, and nearly all of them find those businesses the same way: a quick search on a phone, often from the parking lot. What wins that search is mostly practical.

Say how close you are

"Ten minutes from the Horse Park" or "just off I-20" in plain words on the page tells a visitor more than a map pin alone.

Keep event-weekend hours current

If you open early for a show weekend or the Cherry Blossom Festival, put it on the site and on Google the week before.

Make calling one tap

A visitor with a horse trailer and a flat tire is not going to fill out a long form. The number belongs at the top of every page.

Load fast on a weak signal

Compressed photos and a lean page open quickly even on a crowded event network. Every Dominion site is built that way.
