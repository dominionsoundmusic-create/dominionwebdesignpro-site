Good New Orleans website design starts with the fact that nobody here lives in "New Orleans." They live in the Marigny, Gentilly, Algiers or Uptown, they eat on Freret, they shop on Magazine, and they will tell you which side of Canal Street a place is on before they tell you its name. A visitor in town for a weekend searches the same way, because every guidebook taught them those names. A site that only says "serving the New Orleans area" is the one thing in this city that sounds like it came from nowhere.

Web design New Orleans visitors and neighbors both search by place

Orleans Parish and the City of New Orleans share the same boundary, and the Census Bureau put the population at about 362,000 in its July 2025 estimate. Inside that line are dozens of neighborhoods with names known well past Louisiana, and each one draws a different kind of searcher. The table is how we think about it when we plan a page.

Magazine Street shows why the detail matters. The city's tourism office describes it as about six miles long, running from Canal Street to Audubon Park through the Central Business District, the Garden District and Uptown, with locally owned shops as the rule. "On Magazine" tells a shopper almost nothing about where you are; a cross street and a neighborhood name tell them everything.

The jobs behind the plates and the stages

Hospitality is not a side industry here. Bureau of Labor Statistics data published through FRED counted about 69,400 leisure and hospitality jobs in the New Orleans metro in December 2025, and about 58,800 of them were in accommodation and food services alone. Against roughly 478,000 nonfarm jobs in the metro that month, that works out to about one job in seven. The annual average was 69,000 in 2024, up from about 53,900 in 2020.

Behind those numbers are thousands of owner-run operations: the po-boy counter, the second-line brass band, the walking tour guide with a ghost route, the jeweler selling at a weekend market, the caterer who cooks for weddings and office lunches. Most of them sell through word of mouth and a phone, and plenty have a website that was set up once and never touched again.

Carnival, Jazz Fest and the weeks you get the most strangers

Carnival season opens on Twelfth Night, January 6, and Mardi Gras itself falls on Tuesday, February 9, 2027. The New Orleans Jazz and Heritage Festival is set for two long weekends at the Fair Grounds Race Course, April 22 to 25 and April 29 to May 2, 2027, with the lineup usually announced around December. Those weeks fill the city with people who have never walked past your door, and every one of them checks a phone first. If parades close your block, if you run a festival menu or if a band you book is playing a late set, the home page should say so weeks ahead, and your Google Business Profile should say the same thing.

A typical owner, walked through

Picture a made-up example, not a client: a two-person kitchen on Freret that does lunch, takes catering orders, and books a trio on Friday nights. Freret is a good place to imagine it. The National Trust's Main Street program adopted the corridor in 2001, and by 2011 local food writers were calling the blocks between Napoleon and Jefferson avenues the city's newest restaurant row.

  1. The menu goes on the page as text.Not a photo of a chalkboard. Typed dishes load fast on a phone, read clearly, and can be matched to a search for "shrimp po-boy Uptown."
  2. Catering gets its own page.How many people, how far ahead to order, delivery range by neighborhood, and a form that sends the request straight to the owner's email.
  3. Friday music has a short calendar.Date, start time, cover or no cover. The owner texts new dates, and we post them.
  4. Hours carry the exceptions.Parade days, summer closures, the Monday off. The site and the Google listing stay matched so nobody drives over to a locked door.

A tour guide, a brass band or a clinic Uptown would get a different list, but the method is the same: find out what the customer needs to decide, then put it where a thumb can reach it.

Web design Louisiana owners can pair with free local help

A website is one tool. These groups help with the rest, and we have no tie to any of them.

LSBDC Greater New Orleans Region

The Louisiana Small Business Development Center network is hosted statewide by LSU through ten regional offices. The Greater New Orleans office is hosted by Xavier University of Louisiana and serves Orleans, Jefferson, Plaquemines, St. Bernard, St. Charles, St. James and St. John the Baptist parishes, with an office on North Causeway in Metairie.

New Orleans Chamber of Commerce

Founded in 2004, the chamber runs networking events, education and advocacy for its members, along with groups such as its Women's Business Alliance.

City Office of Supplier Diversity

For owners who want city contracts, this office certifies firms under the State and Local Disadvantaged Business Enterprise program, which is race and gender neutral. Applications go through an online system, and approved firms are listed in a public directory.

Propeller

A local nonprofit that in 2025 picked 15 ventures for the Growth Track of its Impact Accelerator, also runs a Startup Bootcamp for early-stage ventures, and works with Thrive New Orleans and Fund 17 on a Financial Wellness Collaborative of workshops for owners.
