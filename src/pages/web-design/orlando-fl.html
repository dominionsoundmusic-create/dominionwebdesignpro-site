Web design in Orlando has to answer one question before anything else: is your customer a visitor or a neighbor? Visit Orlando reported a record of about 76.7 million visitors in 2025. The people who live here have different errands: they get their hair cut in Audubon Park, eat in Mills 50 and call an air conditioning company when the unit quits in August. Those two crowds search differently, and a website written for one often misses the other.

Dominion Web Design Pro builds sites for Orlando businesses from Big Sandy, East Texas. Maurice Johnson writes every page himself, you get a free demo first, and the price is printed and paid once.

The visitor side of Orlando

{{ img("demo-images/cleaning/2.jpg", "Caddy of cleaning sprays and a folded towel on a gleaming tile floor in a sunny living room", 1344, 768) }}

Visit Orlando counted about 70.3 million domestic visitors in 2025 and 6.3 million international ones, with Canada the largest foreign market and Mexico, Colombia and Japan setting records. Behind that traffic sits a long chain of small businesses: vacation rental cleaners, pool techs who service rental homes, private tour guides, family and wedding photographers, airport shuttle drivers and party rental companies.

Most of their customers find them from a hotel room or a rental house, often the day before they need them. The site has to load fast on hotel Wi-Fi, show prices or a clear way to get one, take a booking or a call in one tap, and say which areas you serve. A photographer who works sunrise sessions, for example, needs the session types, the meeting points and the calendar on the first screen, not on page four.

Plenty of Orlando businesses straddle both. A pressure washing company might clean rental homes for owners who live out of state and driveways for families in Conway. In that case the home page stays general and each audience gets its own page, written for the person who reads it.

The neighborhood side: Orlando's Main Street districts

The City of Orlando has run its Main Streets program since 2008, helping neighborhood commercial districts organize, promote themselves and fill storefronts. The first districts included Audubon Park, College Park, Downtown South (now SoDo), Ivanhoe Village and Mills 50. Later came Church Street, Thornton Park, the Milk District, Gateway Orlando and Curry Ford West, which the city council added as a district in 2018. The program now counts twelve neighborhood commercial districts.

Businesses in these districts live on repeat customers who live nearby. Their websites should read like the shop does: the owner's name, the real hours, what is on the shelf or the menu this month, the parking situation and a district events calendar link. When the Main Street hosts a night market or a festival, a visitor who walked by searches the shop name the next morning, and the site needs to be there.

Writing for Orlando's Spanish-speaking customers

The City of Orlando's summary of the 2023 American Community Survey reports that 34.9% of residents identified as Hispanic or Latino. Pew Research Center found that, as of 2013, the Orlando metro had more Puerto Rican residents than any other metro in Florida, about 314,000. For a plumber, a dental office or an insurance agent working east Orlando or out toward Kissimmee, a share of callers will be more comfortable in Spanish.

That does not mean you need two complete websites. It usually means a handful of Spanish pages for your main services, written or checked by a fluent speaker (you, your staff or someone you trust), with the same phone number and hours as the English ones. The EN/ES button on this site uses Google's machine translation, which is fine for skimming but not how your business should introduce itself. Maurice writes the English and builds the structure; the Spanish wording comes from a fluent person you approve.

Web design in Orlando that turns local searches into calls

Google explains that local results come down to relevance, distance and prominence. Distance works against a visitor-facing business in an odd way: the searcher is standing in a rental home twenty minutes from your shop, so Google leans on how clearly your site says you serve that area. That is the argument for pages that name the towns and corridors you cover, written about each one rather than cloned.

For a Main Street shop the issue is different. You are close to your customers already; what you need is a site that is plainly about what you sell, current, and consistent with your Google Business Profile, which the Pro package sets up. No one can promise you a spot on the map, and we never will. We can make sure Google and your customers both understand what you do and where.

  1. Pick your customer.Visitor, neighbor or both. The answer decides what goes on the first screen.
  2. Name your areas.The districts or towns you serve, each with a reason to be on the page.
  3. Plan the languages.Which pages, if any, need Spanish, and who checks the wording.
  4. Make the call easy.A tap-to-call number and a short form on every page.
