Thirty years on one block is a selling point. Put it on the page.

San Francisco keeps an official list of its long-running small businesses, the Legacy Business Registry. To qualify, a business generally has to have operated in the city for 30 years or more, with no break in its San Francisco operations longer than two years, and it has to have contributed to the history or identity of a neighborhood. A business open more than 20 years can also qualify if the Small Business Commission finds it has made a significant contribution and would face a significant risk of displacement without the listing.

An application needs a nomination from a member of the Board of Supervisors or the mayor. After that the path runs through two bodies: the Historic Preservation Commission hears the application and makes a recommendation, then the Small Business Commission decides. Applying is free. Registry businesses can seek city grants, though the city's own form says a listing does not by itself entitle anyone to grant money.

Here is why that matters for a website. A customer comparing two electricians, two tailors or two delis on a phone has about ten seconds of attention. "Family owned since 1991, on the city's Legacy Business Registry" answers a question that a stock photo and the word "quality" never will. If your shop has that kind of story, it belongs on the home page, not buried on page four or left to a listing someone else wrote.

Web design San Francisco Bay Area customers can find by neighborhood

People in this city rarely say "San Francisco" when they mean where they shop. They say the Mission, the Sunset, the Richmond, Chinatown, North Beach, the Castro or the Bayview, and they search that way too. A vet in the Richmond and a vet in the Bayview are not serving the same households, and a customer who searches by neighborhood wants the one nearby. City corridor programs have long been organized around streets like Mission Street, Clement Street, Irving Street and Third Street, which is a good reminder that the street a shop sits on is part of its address in people's heads.

Web design for the Bay Area beyond the city line

The region is bigger than most owners' service areas. The Association of Bay Area Governments counts nine counties and 101 cities and towns around the bay. A cleaning company in the Bayview may happily drive to Daly City and South San Francisco but never to Walnut Creek. A bookkeeper in Berkeley may take clients from Oakland and the city but not from San Jose. The right website names the places you will go and leaves out the ones you will not, because a call from a town you do not serve is a wasted call for both of you.

  • Neighborhood or city in the page title and the first paragraph, where it is true.
  • Cross streets or the corridor you are on, plus hours and how to get there.
  • A separate page for each outside city you want work from, written about that city.
  • A phone number at the top that dials on one tap.

San Francisco website design and the way Google picks local results

Google says local results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence: how well a business matches the search, how far it is from the searcher or the place named, and how well known it is. A legacy shop often already has prominence it never claimed online, in years of press, word of mouth and listings. Relevance is the part a site controls. Responsive pages that load quickly on a phone, a clear service list, titles and descriptions submitted to Google, and schema markup on Pro all help Google match you to the search. Free help San Francisco owners can use alongside a website

If you are still sorting out permits or a business plan, start with these before you spend money on anything, a website included.

  1. The Office of Small Business.The city's central point of information for small businesses. Its staff work at the Permit Center at 49 South Van Ness Avenue and help owners work out which permits they need, and it offers free counseling.
  2. The San Francisco SBDC.Hosted by the city's Office of Economic and Workforce Development, it gives free, confidential one-on-one consulting and workshops in English, Spanish, Mandarin and Cantonese, and has a restaurant division for food businesses.
  3. SCORE San Francisco and East Bay.Free, confidential mentoring by video, in person or by email, from a chapter based in Oakland that covers the city and much of the East Bay and beyond.
  4. The San Francisco Chamber of Commerce.Founded during the Gold Rush in 1850, it lists more than 900 member companies, and its membership options include individuals and micro-businesses.

And if your business has been open 30 years or more, read the Legacy Business Program pages on sf.gov. The application costs nothing.
