Knoxville TN web design has an unusual job: the same business often sells to two crowds who never meet. One crowd lives here and shops in Bearden, Fountain City and Farragut all year. The other is driving through on the way to the mountains, searching on a phone at a gas pump for a guide, a rental, a meal or someone to clean the cabin before the next family checks in. A site that only speaks to one of them leaves money on the table.

Dominion Web Design Pro writes sites that speak to both. Maurice Johnson builds every page by hand from Big Sandy in East Texas, shows you a free demo of your own site first, and charges one printed price.

The busiest national park sits just over the hill

{{ img("demo-images/landscaping/2.jpg", "Curving stone path through a freshly cut lawn with rounded shrubs and evergreens, leading toward two-story homes in late afternoon sun", 1344, 768) }}

Great Smoky Mountains National Park recorded about 11.5 million visits in 2025, the most of any national park and more than twice the count at Zion, which came second. Most of those visitors pass near Knoxville on their way in or out, and plenty of them need something on the way.

The money is real. State tourism figures put 2024 visitor spending in Sevier County, home to Pigeon Forge and Gatlinburg, at about $3.93 billion, third among Tennessee's 95 counties. Tennessee as a whole counted 147 million visitors and $31.66 billion in direct visitor spending that year.

For a Knoxville owner that means two kinds of searches hit the same website. A visitor types "kayak rental near Knoxville" from a hotel room on a Thursday night. A homeowner in West Knoxville types "landscaper Bearden" on a Saturday morning. Both expect an answer in a few seconds, and both leave if they cannot find the hours or the phone number.

One week in the life of a cabin turnover crew

This is an example, not a client: a made-up two-person cleaning business based in South Knoxville, to show where a website earns its keep.

  1. Monday: the property manager checks you outA rental manager with cabins near Pigeon Forge needs a backup crew. She searches, finds three companies and opens each site. She wants to see the area you cover, whether you do linens and restocking, and how to reach you the same day.
  2. Wednesday: the owner from out of stateSomeone who owns one cabin and lives in Ohio searches at night. He cannot meet you. Your site is the whole interview, so it needs your name, what a turnover includes and a form that lands in your inbox.
  3. Friday: the local clientA family in Fountain City wants a deep clean before company comes. They searched for house cleaning, not cabins. A separate page for residential work is how they find you.
  4. Sunday night: checking messagesEvery lead from the week sits in one email folder, with a name and a number, because the contact form sent each one there.

What visitors need from the page that locals do not

{{ img("demo-images/cleaning/2.jpg", "Bucket of spray bottles and a yellow towel on a shining white tile floor in a sunny, freshly cleaned room with tall arched windows", 1344, 768) }}

A person from Atlanta or Cincinnati does not know how far Maryville is from downtown or how long the drive to the park entrance takes. They do not know your shop closes early on Sundays. So the site has to say it: where you are in plain terms, the hours by season if they change, whether to book ahead, and what to bring. Outfitters and guides should answer the questions they hear at the counter every day, on the page, before anyone has to call.

Locals want different things. They want proof you are still in business, the neighborhoods you cover and a way to get a quote without a long phone call. Writing one page for both readers usually fails both. Writing a page for each kind of customer, and a page for each service, works better and gives Google a clearer picture of what you do.
