Good website design Lexington customers respond to begins with knowing who those customers are. In this city a big share of them are connected to horses. The Thoroughbred farms around town need farriers, vets, fence crews, barn builders, haulers, feed and hay suppliers, insurance agents and bookkeepers, and the people who work on those farms need a mechanic, a dentist and somewhere to eat like everyone else. A Lexington business site has to make sense to a barn manager in a pickup as quickly as it does to a student on campus.

Website design Lexington horse country actually needs

Kentucky's horse business is not a small side industry. The 2012 Kentucky Equine Survey, run with the University of Kentucky, put the state equine industry's total economic impact at almost $3 billion and counted 40,665 jobs tied to it. The researchers described the central Bluegrass as an economic cluster, and they pointed out that the farms lean on a long list of supporting businesses: fencing, feed, bedding, insurance and pharmaceutical suppliers among them.

Most of those supporting businesses are small, and many of them have no website, or a page someone built years ago and forgot. That is a problem, because farm work is booked by people who check before they call. A broodmare farm hiring a new fence contractor wants to see board fence the crew has built. A trainer looking for a hauler wants to know whether you go to Florida for the winter. Nobody wants to dig through a Facebook feed to find out.

The groundskeepers, mowing crews and landscape companies that keep farm entrances and lawns tidy are part of the same chain. Their sites need the same few things: what you do, which roads and counties you cover, photos of real work and a number that rings straight to you.

None of that needs fancy design. It needs someone who asks the right questions and writes the answers down in plain words.

Trades that live off the farms, and what each site has to show

If your work touches horses, a good page answers the question a farm manager is really asking. Here is how that looks trade by trade.

Farriers

Whether you shoe or trim only, which disciplines you work with, how far you travel and how a new client gets on your schedule. Most farriers do not want a booking button; a clear "call or text" works better.

Equine vets

Ambulatory or clinic, the services you offer, emergency coverage and the counties you drive to. A practice page should also say which kinds of horses you see most, so the right callers find you.

Fence and barn builders

Board, plank or wire, stall barns or run-in sheds, and photos of finished jobs with the farm names left off unless the owner agrees. Estimate requests should ask for acreage and location up front.

Horse transport

Local runs, sale-day hauls and long-distance trips, with what your trailers can carry. Seasonal routes belong on their own page so you can update them without touching the rest.

Tack, feed and hay

What you stock, delivery areas, hours and whether you take phone orders. A page with current hours beats a slideshow every time.

Insurance and bookkeeping

Which kinds of farm and equine work you handle, written so an owner can tell in one read whether you fit, plus the license or credential details a careful buyer will look for.

Two meets, a big sale, and buyers from out of state

Keeneland holds two race meets a year: a spring meet in April and a fall meet in October. Its September Yearling Sale is the largest yearling auction in North America, the November Breeding Stock Sale follows, and a January sale for horses of all ages rounds out the year.

A 2024 study by Thomas Lambert of the University of Louisville estimated Keeneland's economic impact on Fayette and the surrounding counties at about $1.6 billion for 2023, most of it from the auctions. The same study found that about 64% of racing patrons and about 67% of auction attendees and participants came from outside Kentucky.

Think about what that means for a small business. A buyer's agent flying in for the September sale does not know which restaurant near the sales pavilion takes a group of eight, who details rental cars, or which vet can do a pre-purchase exam on short notice. They search on a phone from a hotel room. If your page says what you do and that you are ten minutes from the track or downtown, you are in the running. If it says only "serving Central Kentucky," you are a guess.

Web design Lexington KY customers can follow, from Hamburg to Southland

Lexington's past as farm country shows up in its shopping streets. Hamburg, on the east side, started as Hamburg Place, a horse farm John Madden bought in 1898 and named for the horse whose sale paid for it. In the late 1990s the core of the farm was developed into Hamburg Pavilion along I-75 and Man o' War Boulevard, and streets there still carry the names of horses. The farm's horse cemetery survives inside the development, moved a few hundred yards from its first site.

  • Chevy ChaseOne of the city's oldest shopping districts, centered on Euclid Avenue and home to owner-run boutiques and the Chevy Chase Inn, the city's oldest bar.
  • Southland DriveA retail corridor of local restaurants and shops, where a city streetscape project finished around 2020 added more than 120 trees and new sidewalks on both sides of the street.
  • Downtown and the Distillery DistrictOffices, restaurants, bars and event spaces close together, where visitors and office workers both search on their phones.
  • Nicholasville and GeorgetownThe neighboring towns to the south and north. Many Lexington trades work both, and each deserves its own page if you really take jobs there.

Google says local results depend mostly on relevance, distance and prominence. Naming the area you serve plainly, and writing a real paragraph about working there, gives the search engine something relevant to match. Nobody can promise a ranking, and we will not.

The University of Kentucky crowd

The other half of the city's customer base walks around campus. UK reported a record fall 2025 enrollment of 38,719 students in its preliminary count, up from 35,951 the year before. Add faculty, hospital staff and visiting families, and you have a lot of people who know nothing about your part of town and look everything up.

For a coffee shop, a barber, a tutor or a moving company, that means a site that loads fast on a phone, shows hours that are actually current, and says how far you are from campus. It also means updating the site when a semester starts or a home game shifts traffic. While you are on our hosting plan, you send the change and it gets made.

Help in Lexington that costs little or nothing

Kentucky is a small business state: the SBA Office of Advocacy's 2025 profile counted 393,860 small businesses there, nearly all of the businesses in the state. Several groups in town help them.

  • Kentucky Small Business Development Center. Administered by UK's College of Agriculture, Food and Environment as an accredited partner of the U.S. Small Business Administration, with a Bluegrass office in Lexington for one-on-one advising.
  • Commerce Lexington. The chamber runs an Access Loan Program, started in 2001 with local lenders, that has helped secure more than $25 million in loans for startups and expansions across 19 counties, plus a Community and Minority Business Development division and free Money Smart for Small Business classes.
  • SCORE Kentucky. Volunteer mentors who serve Lexington as well as Louisville, free for the life of the business.

Whatever you decide about a website, a free hour with one of these advisors is worth taking first.
