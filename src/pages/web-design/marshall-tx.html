A festival that started as a newspaper column

In 1987 Marshall was in a hard spot, with unemployment near twenty percent. George S. Smith, then editor of the Marshall News Messenger, walked across downtown, pictured the Harrison County Courthouse strung with lights, and wrote about it. A Marshall native living in France answered with a check for $25,000, and the town built the rest. Today the courthouse is wrapped in lights every holiday season, and the festival has grown to include an ice rink and a historic carousel.

The story matters to a business owner because it shows how Marshall works: the town decided to bring people downtown, and it did. Every year since, visitors who have never been here walk the square with phones in their hands. The shops, restaurants and inns they can find online get their business. The ones they cannot find do not.

Who is searching for Marshall businesses

Marshall had 23,392 residents at the 2020 census, but its customer base is much wider than its population. The town was founded in 1841 and named for Chief Justice John Marshall, and it has been a crossroads ever since. The first telegraph office in Texas opened here in 1854. The Texas and Pacific Railway later made Marshall a railroad town, and Amtrak's Texas Eagle still stops at the depot. Marshall also sits on Interstate 20, between Shreveport to the east and Longview and Dallas to the west.

Put together, that gives a Marshall shop an unusually mixed crowd: people who live here, festival visitors, interstate travelers, day-trippers from Louisiana and business travelers in town for a few nights. They have one thing in common. None of them will find you by driving past.

What a first-time visitor needs from your page

Picture a couple from Shreveport parked near the courthouse on a December evening. They have twenty minutes before the carousel line gets long, and they want dinner. They will open two or three sites, and they will leave any of them that makes them work. Here is what keeps them on yours.

  • Open or closed, today. Hours at the top of the page, with holiday changes posted as soon as you know them.
  • How far from the square. A sentence like "two blocks north of the courthouse" beats a map pin a stranger has to zoom into.
  • What it costs, roughly. A menu, a price range or a few example prices, so they know you fit what they planned to spend.
  • A way to call in one tap. Big enough for a cold thumb, at the top of every page.
  • Real photos. Your dining room, your shelves, your work. Stock pictures make visitors wonder what you are hiding.

None of that is fancy, and that is the point. The same page also works for the regular who drives in from Hallsville on a Tuesday in March, which is the customer who pays the bills the other ten months of the year.
