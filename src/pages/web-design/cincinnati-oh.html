What a market stall teaches about a website

Findlay Market opened in 1852 on land donated by the estate of General James Findlay, and it is Ohio's oldest continuously operated public market, the last of the nine public markets that once served Cincinnati. The market says it has more than 50 full-time merchants and welcomes more than a million visitors a year.

Think about how a good stall works. The sign says what you sell. The price is on the card. The hours are posted. A regular knows to come early on Saturday for the best cuts. A website for a vendor or a small shop should work the same way: what you sell, what it costs or where to ask, when you are open, where exactly to find you inside or on the block, and what is good this week. Most vendor websites skip half of that, or never get updated after the first month.

Over-the-Rhine has two audiences, and your site should speak to both

Over-the-Rhine is Cincinnati's oldest neighborhood, with more than 900 Italianate buildings. Its turnaround on Vine Street began in 2004, when the nonprofit developer 3CDC began buying run-down buildings and pairing street-level retail with apartments and condos above. The work has since moved north, and a roughly $100 million North OTR effort reported in 2026 includes new housing, a park and Vine Street streetscape work. The changes have also drawn criticism over displacement, and the neighborhood's longtime residents are still part of it.

For a business there, that means two kinds of customer. One lives a few blocks away and needs hours, prices and whether you deliver. The other is visiting for a game, a show or a night out and needs to know what you are, how long the wait is and where to park. A good OTR site answers both on the first screen of a phone without making either one dig.

  • Hours that match your door, updated when they change.
  • The street and cross street, plus a parking or transit note.
  • A short paragraph on who runs the place, written with you.
  • Photos of the real room, the real counter and the real food or work.

A city of neighborhood business districts

Cincinnati is a city of about 311,000 people by the Census Bureau's 2023 estimate, split into 52 neighborhoods, and many of them have a business district of their own. The city backs those districts directly: its Department of Community and Economic Development runs the Neighborhood Business District Improvement Program, which funds facade work, parking, building renovation and streetscapes through recognized neighborhood business groups.

Each district has a different feel. Hyde Park Square is ringed by shops, boutiques, cafes and restaurants, with a Sunday farmers market in season. Oakley's district centers on the Geier Esplanade, known as Oakley Square, with the Twentieth Century Theatre built in 1941. Northside, Walnut Hills and Pleasant Ridge have their own strips and their own regulars, and the suburbs stretch north to West Chester. Across the river, Covington and Newport belong to the same region and the same commute.

A salon on Hyde Park Square and a salon in Northside are both salons, but their customers search with different words and drive from different places. The site should name the square or the street, not just "Cincinnati."

Big headquarters downtown, small businesses all around them

Cincinnati is a corporate headquarters town. Procter & Gamble has had its headquarters in downtown Cincinnati since 1837, and Kroger, the country's largest pure-play grocer, is based in the city too. Those offices bring workers downtown every weekday, and those workers need lunch, haircuts, dry cleaning, car repair and someone to fix the water heater at home.

A small business does not have to sell to a corporation to benefit. It has to be easy to find when one of those employees searches on a lunch break or on the drive home. That comes down to clear pages, a phone number that dials with one tap and a contact form that actually reaches you.

How Cincinnati customers find a business on Google

Google says local results depend on relevance, distance and prominence. Distance is fixed by where the searcher stands. Prominence builds over time with reviews, mentions and links. Relevance is what your website controls: whether it says, in plain words, which jobs you do and which neighborhoods you cover. A plumber whose site says "drain cleaning in Westwood and Price Hill" gives Google something to match. One that says "quality service, call today" does not. No honest builder promises a ranking, and we will not either.
