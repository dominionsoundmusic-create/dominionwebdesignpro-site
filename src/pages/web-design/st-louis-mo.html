One name, two governments and dozens of towns

The split is old. In 1876 St. Louis voters chose to separate the city from St. Louis County, and the city has been an independent city, belonging to no county, ever since. The split was made possible by Missouri's 1875 constitution, and undoing it means going through the state constitution again. The most recent big attempt to reunite the two, the Better Together plan, was withdrawn in 2019.

The result today is a city of about 278,000 people beside a county of just under one million, according to Census Bureau estimates for 2025. The county is not one place either. It holds 88 municipalities, from large cities such as Florissant and Chesterfield to small villages, and more than 300,000 residents live in unincorporated areas such as Affton, Lemay and Spanish Lake that get local services from the county itself.

Locals add their own layer on top. People talk about North County, South County, West County and Mid County, and those labels are informal: one rule of thumb cited by Fox 2 sorts towns by the interstates, with Ferguson, Florissant and Hazelwood to the north and Affton, Crestwood and Sunset Hills to the south. Nobody types "unincorporated Mid County" into a search bar. They type the name they use at the kitchen table.

What a St. Louis website design has to name

If customers search by the place they call home, your pages have to use those names. Here is how that plays out for different kinds of St. Louis businesses.

Shops tied to one neighborhood

A bakery on The Hill, a bar in Soulard or a taqueria on Cherokee Street lives on its block. The site needs the neighborhood name, the cross streets, parking, hours and a map, so a visitor who searched the neighborhood finds you and knows how to get there.

Trades that cross the city line

A roofer or plumber may work South City on Monday and Webster Groves on Tuesday. That business needs a page for each area it wants work in, written about that area, not one page with a list of twenty town names pasted at the bottom.

Offices that draw from a whole county side

A dentist in West County or a tax preparer in North County serves several municipalities at once. The about and contact pages should say which ones, and how far people usually drive to reach you.

Neighborhoods with their own pull

The City of St. Louis counts 79 neighborhoods, and several of them are destinations in their own right. The Hill is known for its Italian American heritage, restaurants and bakeries. Soulard, one of the oldest neighborhoods, is known for its brick row houses, its corner bars and a long-running farmers market. Cherokee Street has the city's best known stretch of Mexican shops and restaurants west of Jefferson Avenue, with antique stores to the east. The Grove is the business strip at the heart of Forest Park Southeast, known for restaurants and nightlife.

A business in one of these places gets two kinds of searches: people looking for the neighborhood itself ("restaurants in Soulard") and people looking for the service nearby. Naming the neighborhood plainly in your page title and first paragraph helps with both. So does a short paragraph about where you are, written by someone who asked you, not lifted from a tourism site.

Website design St. Louis searchers can find starts with the place name

Google says local results turn mainly on three things: relevance, or how well a listing matches the search; distance from the searcher or the place named in the search; and prominence, how well known the business is. When someone searches "plumber Kirkwood," distance and relevance both lean on that town name. If your site and your Google Business Profile never mention Kirkwood, you are asking Google to guess.

This is where St. Louis website design differs from a city laid out as one big municipality. In a place with 88 county municipalities plus city neighborhoods, the number of names a customer might type is huge, and most small businesses only need a handful of them. The job is to pick the right ones and write a page that earns each: what you do there, how quickly you can be there, and what is different about working in that part of town. Nobody can promise a ranking, and we will not. What we can do is make your site clearly about your work in the places you name.

Help that already exists for St. Louis owners

A website is one piece. These organizations offer advice and money for the rest, mostly at no cost:

  • St. Louis Development Corporation (SLDC). The city's economic development agency runs a Business Assistance Center for people starting a business in the city and makes small business loans through the St. Louis Local Development Company. The city's Community Development Administration runs a commercial facade improvement program.
  • Missouri Small Business Development Center. Part of the University of Missouri system, with an office in downtown St. Louis offering one-on-one consulting and training.
  • SCORE St. Louis. Volunteer mentors and workshops for Greater St. Louis, Southwest Missouri and Southern Illinois.

Missouri is a small business state by the numbers. The SBA Office of Advocacy counted 579,040 small businesses in Missouri in its 2024 profile, 99.5% of all businesses in the state, employing 44.9% of the state's workers.
