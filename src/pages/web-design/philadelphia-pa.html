A good Philadelphia web designer has to understand two things about how this city does business. First, people shop by the corridor: East Passyunk, Germantown Avenue, Main Street in Manayunk, the blocks of Fishtown and Northern Liberties. Second, the city runs on paperwork, and customers increasingly check it. Every business here needs a Commercial Activity License, contractors carry license numbers the city expects to see on their advertising, and the storefront itself may have been fixed up with city money. A site that shows where you sit on your corridor and which licenses you hold answers questions most competitors leave open.

A city of about 1.57 million that shops street by street

The Census Bureau put Philadelphia at 1,574,281 residents in July 2025. Those residents do much of their everyday buying on neighborhood commercial corridors, and there are a lot of them: years ago the city's Commerce director put the count at around 265. Today the Department of Commerce still funds community groups to hire corridor managers and pays for corridor cleaning through its PHL Taking Care of Business program.

Each corridor has its own character, and that character is what a visitor searches for. A few examples:

The license numbers a Philadelphia website should show

Philadelphia asks every person or company doing business in the city, including out-of-town businesses, to hold a Commercial Activity License. It costs nothing and is issued online through the city's eCLIPSE system. Trades carry more. Contractors who do construction, demolition or repair work need a city contractor license unless they work only on existing one- and two-family homes, and those home improvement contractors register with the Pennsylvania Attorney General's office instead. Plumbing, electrical and fire suppression work need a city trade license either way.

The city also says contractors must show their Contractor License Number on advertisements, stationery, proposals, job sites and company vehicles. Homeowners are told to check that a contractor is licensed before hiring. So the number is not just a rule to follow; it is a reason to trust you, and it should be easy to find.

  • Your contractor license or home improvement registration number in the footer of every page.
  • Your trade license type on the about page, in plain words: licensed plumber, licensed electrician.
  • The neighborhoods and suburbs you work in, so a homeowner knows you cover their block.
  • A quote form that asks for the address and the job, and emails it straight to you.

A new storefront deserves new photos online

The city's Storefront Improvement Program, run by the Department of Commerce, reimburses up to half the cost of exterior work for businesses and property owners on designated commercial corridors. As of June 2026 the cap is $20,000 for a single property and $30,000 for a corner or multi-address property, and the city says the program has now helped more than 1,000 businesses. Eligible work includes masonry, painting, windows, doors, lighting, signs and awnings, and the work has to be approved before it starts.

If you go through it, finish the job online too. The photos on your website and your Google Business Profile are often the first look a customer gets at your front door. A fresh facade with a five-year-old photo of the old one sends a mixed message. We build sites with a gallery and an about section that are easy to update when the scaffolding comes down.

Keep the paperwork on your site current

Licenses get renewed, hours change, and a domain name expires if nobody watches the date. A site that still shows last year's license number or the old holiday hours costs trust quietly.
