Web design in Amarillo has an unusual job. Interstate 40 brings a steady stream of people across the Panhandle who have never heard of you and need food, fuel, tires, a room or a repair within the next exit or two. Meanwhile the people who live here pick a dentist, a mechanic or a caterer once and stick with them. Most local businesses serve some of both, and the same website has to work for each.

Dominion Web Design Pro is Maurice Johnson, a Texan in Big Sandy who writes each page himself. You get a free demo, and the price is a single payment.

Web design Amarillo TX businesses need for two audiences at once

The old Route 66 still runs through town along Amarillo Boulevard, Sixth Avenue and Ninth Avenue, while I-40 carries today's cross-country traffic. A family pulling off the interstate is tired and in a hurry. They are on a phone, maybe on a weak signal, and they decide in seconds. A heavy page with a slideshow and a pop-up loses them to the next result.

The regular customer reads differently. They want to know who you are, how long you have been at that location, what you charge, and whether you will still answer when something goes wrong in six months. Both readers want the same basics: hours, location, phone. After that, the traveler wants speed and the neighbor wants trust.

Writing for each reader on the same page

The driver passing through

  • Distance from the interstate and the closest exit, in the first lines
  • Open now, and how late, without scrolling
  • Room for an RV, a trailer or a big rig, if you have it
  • A short menu or price list as text, never a slow PDF
  • A number that dials with one tap

The Amarillo regular

  • Who owns the business and who does the work
  • Full services, explained in plain words
  • Google reviews shown on the site (included in Pro)
  • The neighborhoods or nearby towns you cover
  • A form for questions that are not urgent

We build the traveler's answers into the top of the home page and give the regular deeper pages to read. Nothing is hidden behind a menu the driver will never open.

Cattle, beef and medicine: who the locals are

Away from the highway, the Amarillo economy leans on agriculture. The Amarillo Economic Development Corporation describes the region as one of the top beef-producing areas in the nation, with many ranches and feedlots in and around the city and a large Tyson beef plant in town. That work supports a long list of smaller businesses: truck and trailer repair, welding, veterinary practices, feed and equipment suppliers, and the restaurants that feed shift workers.

Health care is the other pillar. The Harrington Regional Medical Center complex holds three acute care hospitals with nearly 1,000 beds, plus rehabilitation and skilled nursing facilities, and Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center has run an Amarillo campus there since 1994. Clinics, therapists, pharmacies and dental offices cluster around it, and their patients come from across the Panhandle.

Amarillo places worth naming on your site

Google ranks local results on relevance, distance and prominence. Naming the area you work in, in words people actually use, helps on the first two. A few that come up often:

  • Route 66 on SixthThe U.S. Route 66 and Sixth Street Historic District is a 13-block stretch of highway-era storefronts in the San Jacinto area, listed on the National Register in 1994 and now full of antique shops, restaurants and specialty stores.
  • The Medical CenterThe Harrington Regional Medical Center complex, where patients and visiting families look for food, lodging and pharmacies nearby.
  • Canyon and Palo DuroSouth of the city, Palo Duro Canyon State Park covers about 29,000 acres and is often called the second largest canyon in the country, which brings hikers and campers through the area.
  • Amarillo BoulevardPart of the historic Route 66 path through town, lined with older motels and roadside businesses.
