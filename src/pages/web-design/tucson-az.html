A food city built on small kitchens

In December 2015 UNESCO named Tucson a City of Gastronomy, the first city in the United States to get that title. The case leaned on the region's roughly 4,000 years of continuous farming, its work saving native seed lines, and how much of the local economy runs on food jobs and food businesses.

You can see that economy on the street. Mercado San Agustín, a small plaza west of downtown modeled on Mexican marketplaces, was set up partly to incubate small businesses, and several local food brands grew there before opening more locations. Historic Fourth Avenue, just northeast of downtown, counts more than 140 locally owned shops, bars and cafes. Most of these businesses are run by the owner, with no marketing department and no time to fight with a website builder after a twelve-hour shift.

What a Tucson food business site has to get right

A hungry person deciding where to eat gives your site a few seconds. These are the pieces we put first, and why.

The menu as real text

Every dish typed onto the page, with prices if you want them shown, not a photo of a laminated menu or a PDF that takes ten seconds to open on a cell signal. Text loads fast, reads well at any screen size, and is something Google can match to a search like "carne seca near me."

Hours that are actually current

Summer hours, holiday closures, the Monday you shut for a family event. If the site and your Google Business Profile disagree, the customer believes neither. We keep them matched, and changes are part of maintenance.

A catering and events page

Office lunches, quinceañeras, weddings, university events. A separate page that says what you cater, for how many people, how far ahead to book, and a form that sends the request straight to your email.

A food truck adds one more piece: a weekly stop schedule near the top of the home page. Tortillerias, bakeries and small producers who sell through other stores need a short "where to buy" list. Neither needs an online store with a shopping cart, and we will not sell you one you do not need.

Tucson website design that names the right part of town

Google says local results depend on relevance, distance and prominence: how well your listing matches the search, how far you are from the person searching or the place they typed, and how well known you are. Distance is the one you cannot fake, but you can make it clear. A diner on Oracle Road, a shop near the University of Arizona and a caterer who delivers out to the east side are each reaching different people, and the site should say where you are and how far you go.

The metro also spreads well past city limits. Pima County's incorporated places are Tucson, Oro Valley, Marana, Sahuarita and South Tucson, and fast-growing Vail is still unincorporated after several failed votes to become a town. Someone in Marana may never type "Tucson" at all. If you serve those places, a short, honest section about each one helps; a list of fifty suburbs pasted at the bottom of a page does not.

Not a restaurant? The same rules apply

Food is the clearest example, but the habits carry over to every owner-run Tucson business. An auto repair shop on Speedway needs its hours, the makes it works on and a quick way to book a drop-off. A veterinary clinic needs its after-hours plan stated plainly, since a worried pet owner will search at night. A salon, a tax preparer or an HVAC company needs the same three things a kitchen does: say what you do, say when you are open, and make calling or booking a single tap.

Whatever your trade, Maurice asks the questions before writing. What do customers call to ask over and over? Where do they come from? What do they misunderstand about your prices? Those answers become the pages, so the site does some of the explaining you do at the counter now.

Help for Pima County owners that costs little or nothing

  • Pima Community College Small Business Development Center. Part of the SBA-funded SBDC network, it offers no-cost one-on-one counseling and low-cost workshops for businesses in Pima and Santa Cruz counties, covering business plans, licensing, financing and marketing.
  • SCORE Southern Arizona. Volunteer mentors, many of them former owners and executives, give free, confidential advice and run training sessions.
  • City of Tucson Business Services. The city requires a business license for businesses operating in Tucson, and its license team answers questions by phone, email and at the counter downtown. Get this done before you print a menu with your address on it.
  • Tucson Metro Chamber. A member-based business advocacy group whose annual Copper Cactus Awards recognize locally owned Southern Arizona businesses.
