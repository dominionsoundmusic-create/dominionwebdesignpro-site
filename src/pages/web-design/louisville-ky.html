For about two weeks before the first Saturday in May, the Kentucky Derby Festival fills the riverfront and the streets, downtown hotels fill up and visitors look up places to eat, drink and get their car fixed on a phone. Then it ends, and the regulars come back. A website that cannot change as fast as the town does starts telling customers the wrong thing.

Louisville web design that expects the spring rush

The 2026 Kentucky Derby Festival opened with Thunder Over Louisville on Saturday, April 18, and ran more than seventy events before Derby 152 on May 2: a waterfront festival, a balloon race, the miniMarathon and marathon, the Pegasus Parade, the Great Steamboat Race and more. Tourism officials put the 2023 Derby's economic impact above $401 million, with downtown hotels close to 98% full.

That money reaches far past Churchill Downs. It lands at the barber who opens early on Oaks day, the caterer booked for a dozen Derby parties, the cleaner turning over short-term rentals between guests, and the HVAC tech whose phone rings when a rental's air quits with a full house. Each of them needs the website to say something different in late April than it said in February.

For a turnover cleaner, that might be a booking page that opens extra slots for race weekend and closes them the following Monday. For a restaurant, it is a notice with festival hours and whether you take reservations. For a contractor, it might be nothing more than a line that says you are still answering calls that week.

None of this needs a complicated system. It needs a site built with room for a seasonal notice, and someone who will post it when you ask.

Whiskey Row and the businesses that live off bourbon visitors

Before Prohibition, a stretch of Main Street downtown held dozens of distillers, wholesalers and other spirits businesses, the heart of Kentucky's whiskey trade. The name Whiskey Row stuck. The revival is recent: the Evan Williams Bourbon Experience opened on West Main in 2013, and distilleries and tasting rooms have filled in around it. The city's Urban Bourbon Trail, started in 2008, steers visitors to bourbon bars and restaurants across town.

Most of the people who benefit do not make whiskey. They run the tour van, the boutique a block away, the photographer hired for a bachelorette weekend, the restaurant whose bourbon list is the reason a visitor walks in. A visitor deciding where to go next is standing on a sidewalk with a phone, so the page needs your address, hours, a tap-to-call number and a few honest photos near the top, not a slideshow that takes ten seconds to load.

Louisville website design starts with the names customers use

Ask someone where they live and you rarely hear "Louisville." You hear the Highlands, Germantown, Old Louisville, St. Matthews, Jeffersontown, the West End. Google says it ranks local results on relevance, distance and prominence, and a page that plainly names the area you serve gives it something relevant to match.

  • NuLuThe East Market District, known for galleries, antique and specialty shops and restaurants that lean on local farms. Shops here sell as much to visitors as to neighbors.
  • The HighlandsBardstown Road runs through it, with a long commercial stretch of restaurants, bars and owner-run shops that locals call Restaurant Row.
  • Germantown and ButchertownOld neighborhoods just east of downtown. Butchertown was the city's meatpacking hub in the 1800s and now mixes old industry with new bars, a brandy distillery and the soccer stadium.
  • Across the OhioJeffersonville, Clarksville and New Albany sit on the Indiana bank. One Southern Indiana serves as the chamber for Clark and Floyd counties there.

For a plumber who works from Okolona to Prospect, or a dog groomer who draws from both sides of the river, this is where city pages earn their keep. The Pro package includes ten of them, each researched and written about that place instead of copied with a new name.
