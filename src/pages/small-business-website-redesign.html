Small business website redesign, done without breaking what works

A small business website redesign has two jobs. The obvious one is a site that looks current, reads clearly and works on a phone. The less obvious one is not throwing away what the old site already earned: the pages Google knows about, the links other sites point at, and the bookmarks your regular customers use.

Plenty of redesigns get the first job right and the second one wrong. The new site launches, the old addresses quietly turn into error pages, and calls from search slow down for months. We plan the second job before the first one starts.

Everything else about the work matches a new site here. You see a free demo of the redesign first. Maurice Johnson writes the pages by hand. You own the finished site, the domain and the logins.

Signs your site needs a rebuild, not a touch-up

Some sites need new words and a few fixes. Others are past the point where patching helps. These are the signs that usually mean a full rebuild is the cheaper path in the long run:

  • It is painful on a phone. You pinch and scroll sideways to read it, or the menu covers half the screen.
  • You cannot change it. Every edit needs a vendor, a plugin that no longer updates, or a login nobody remembers.
  • It describes a business you used to run. Old services, towns you stopped covering, a phone number that forwards twice.
  • It is one page holding everything. Every service and every town on a single scroll, so nothing gets its own page in search.
  • It loads slowly. Heavy sliders, huge photos and scripts from tools you stopped paying for.
  • Leads disappear. The form sends to a dead inbox, or there is no form at all.
  • It is locked inside a platform. A subscription builder or vendor system you cannot take with you if you leave.

If only one or two of these are true and the site is otherwise sound, say so when you ask for the demo. Sometimes the honest recommendation is to fix the words and keep the rest.

How redesign traffic gets protected

Google's documentation on site moves is direct about this: map each old URL to its new equivalent and use permanent redirects. Here is how that works in practice.

  1. List every old addressEvery page the current site has, plus any address Google still shows for it. Nothing is deleted before it is on the list.
  2. Match each one to its new homeThe old "services" page points at the new services page, an old article at the guide that replaced it. Google warns against sending everything to the home page, which it can treat as an error.
  3. Set up permanent 301 redirectsEach old address answers with a permanent redirect straight to its match, with no chains of hops in between. Google's site move guide says permanent redirects like these do not cause a loss in PageRank.
  4. Keep page addresses where they already workIf an old page's address is fine, the new page keeps it, so there is nothing to redirect at all.
  5. Submit the new sitemap and testThe sitemap goes to Google and the old addresses are checked one by one after launch. Google advises keeping redirects in place for at least a year; we leave ours in place.

Own your domain before anything else changes

The most painful redesign stories start with a domain registered in a former vendor's name. The business paid for it for years and still cannot point it anywhere without permission.

The fix is usually less dramatic than it sounds. Your domain lives at a registrar. ICANN, the body that sets the rules for domain names, states that moving a domain between registrars is the registrant's right, and the current registrar must provide the authorization code for the transfer, either through your account or within five calendar days of a request. A registrar may charge a transfer fee, but it cannot refuse the transfer because you did not pay that fee.

The U.S. Small Business Administration recommends buying domains and web services in the company's name, not an individual's, and that is how we set it up. Your domain stays registered to you. We need access to point it at the new site, and that is all.

Before a redesign, find these four things: who the domain is registered to, where it is registered, who has the hosting login, and which email address receives your contact form. If you do not know, that is fine. Finding out is part of the work.

Keeping the good parts of the old site

A redesign should not mean starting from a blank page. Most older small business sites hold something worth keeping: an accurate history of the business, a clear explanation of a service, photos of real work, the answer to a question customers ask every week.

Maurice reads the old site page by page before writing the new one. Accurate facts and good sentences move across. Outdated claims, filler and anything that cannot be backed up are dropped. Each page is then rewritten for the search it should answer, so the new site is clearer and more specific than the old one, not just newer.

If you have a page that brings in calls today, tell us. That page keeps its address, and its words change only where they need to.
