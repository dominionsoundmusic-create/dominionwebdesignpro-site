The answer first

The best small business website examples do not share a look. They share a job: a stranger lands on the page, understands within a few seconds what the business does and where, and can call or ask for a quote without hunting. A good bakery site and a good roofing site can look nothing alike and still pass every test on this page.

So rather than a gallery of pretty sites, this post gives you two things you can use: where to find real examples worth studying, and a short checklist that tells you whether an example is actually any good. Use it on competitors, on templates, and on your own site.

Where to find examples that are worth copying

The most useful examples are the ones your own customers already see. Search Google the way a customer would, with your trade and your town, and open the websites of the businesses that appear in the map results. You are looking at what you compete against, in your market, today.

Google says its local results are mainly based on relevance, meaning how well a Business Profile matches what someone searched for; distance from the searcher; and how well known the business is, which Google calls prominence. Google also says businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show up. A website does not replace any of that, but it is where people go next, and it is where they decide whether to call.

Template galleries from website builders are the other common source. They are fine for seeing layouts and color ideas. Just remember a template is a demonstration of the builder, not a real business that has to win real customers, so it will not tell you what makes your phone ring.

You can also run any example through our free Site Check to see its page titles, descriptions and basic technical setup side by side with your own.

What the good examples have in common

Open five strong local business sites in a row and the same parts keep showing up, whatever the trade.

  • The top of the home page answers three questions. What do you do, where do you do it, and how do I reach you. The business name, a plain headline, the towns served and a phone number show up before any scrolling.
  • The phone number is a button. On a phone, tapping it starts a call. It is real text, not part of a picture, and it appears on every page.
  • Each main service has its own page. People search for "drain cleaning" or "wedding cakes", not for a list of everything you do. A page per service gives each search a clear answer.
  • The about page sounds like a person. Who runs the place, how long the crew has worked together, what they will and will not take on. Facts the owner can stand behind, not a mission statement.
  • The contact page is complete. Hours, the area served, the address if customers visit, and a form that sends to an inbox someone actually reads.
  • The photos are of the business. The shop, the trucks, the work, so a visitor can see who they would be hiring.

None of this is about style. A plain site with all six beats a beautiful one missing the phone button.

Run these on a competitor's site, a template or your own. The standards come from Google and the U.S. Department of Justice.

Google calls the three speed measures its Core Web Vitals and says it highly recommends site owners achieve good scores "for success with Search and to ensure a great user experience generally." That is not a promise of a ranking for anyone, including us. It is simply the bar Google has published.

The change every example now has to pass: Chrome's https warning

In October 2025, Google's Chrome security team announced it would turn on a setting called "Always Use Secure Connections" by default in October 2026. On October 21, 2026, Google confirmed it had: Chrome now warns before opening a site over plain http, for all users by default. People who had turned on Chrome's Enhanced Protection mode of Safe Browsing got the warning earlier in 2026. Once a visitor approves a site, Chrome does not warn again unless they have stayed away from it for at least two weeks.

For a small business, that means an old site still running on plain http now greets first-time visitors in Chrome with a warning screen before they see the business name. Some will click through. Others may simply tap back to the results and pick another business. When you look at examples now, check the address bar first. If an example you admire still loads without https, it has a problem its owner may not know about yet.

The fix is a security certificate on the site, plus making sure every page and image loads over https. Ask your host whether a certificate is included in your plan. If you are not sure who hosts your site or who could make that change, our guide to what a website costs per month explains what hosting should include.

The part most examples skip: people who cannot use a mouse or see color well

The Department of Justice's web accessibility guidance, published in March 2022 and still posted on ADA.gov, says the ADA requires businesses open to the public to provide full and equal enjoyment of their goods and services to people with disabilities, and that this includes what they offer on the web. The guidance itself is not legally binding, but it shows how the department reads the law.

Its list of common barriers reads like a review of a typical small business site: poor color contrast, images with no alt text, videos with no captions, and navigation that only works with a mouse. Each one is easy to check on any example. Try reading the light gray text, turn off images in your head and ask whether the page still makes sense, and press the Tab key to see whether you can reach the menu and the contact button.

Good examples pass these quietly. Most visitors never notice, and the ones who need it can use the site.
