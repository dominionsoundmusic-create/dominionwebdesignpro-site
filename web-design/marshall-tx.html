Marshall has a two-month season most towns would trade anything for

Wonderland of Lights runs from late November to New Year's Day, wraps the 1901 Harrison County Courthouse and the whole downtown square in millions of white lights, and draws visitors in numbers that dwarf the town's twenty-three thousand residents.

The origin of it is worth knowing, because it says something about the place. In 1987 Marshall was in the worst local economy in its history and unemployment had hit twenty percent. George S. Smith, then editor of the Marshall News Messenger, walked across the square, pictured the courthouse strung with lights, went back to the newsroom and wrote it up. The town built it from there. It now includes an ice rink, a restored vintage carousel, carriage rides, a Christmas market and Santa's Village.

Two months of visitors, every year, walking around your downtown with a phone in their hand looking for somewhere to eat and something to buy. If you own a business on or near that square and you cannot be found in a search, that season is happening to somebody else.

And it is not the only one. The square also hosts the Fire Ant Festival, Market on the Square and Taco Fest through the year.

An old town with more traffic passing through than you would think

Marshall was founded in 1841 as the seat of Harrison County and grew fast on cotton. By 1860 it was the fourth-largest city in Texas. It got the first telegraph line in the state in 1854, when the local paper strung a wire to New Orleans, and later became the westernmost division headquarters of the Texas and Pacific Railway — which is why the brick streets, the grand houses and the 1912 depot all look the way they do.

That history is not just decoration for a business owner. Marshall sits on Interstate 20 and US 59, about forty miles west of Shreveport and a hundred and fifty east of Dallas, and Amtrak's Texas Eagle still stops here. It is also known for its role in the federal patent courts, which brings in out-of-town attorneys and business travel that has nothing to do with tourism.

So your customer base is unusually mixed for a town this size: locals, festival visitors, interstate traffic, Louisiana day-trippers and business travellers. They have exactly one thing in common. None of them will find you by driving past.

All three are fixable, and none of them require you to learn anything.

Getting found in Marshall

Ranking in a town this size is a different job from ranking in Houston. Far less competition, which is good, and far fewer searches, which means each one counts. Three things do most of the work:

  • Your Google Business Profile, filled in and reviewed. For anything typed with "near me" attached, the map results sit above everything else and reviews largely decide the order. It is free, and around here a handful of reviews can put you at the top. Almost nobody bothers.
  • Pages naming what you do and where you do it — Marshall, Hallsville, Waskom, Jefferson, Karnack, Longview — the places you actually serve.
  • Loading fast on a phone. Most of your traffic is somebody standing in a parking lot. Five seconds and they are gone.

Questions people here ask

Our business really only makes money in November and December. Is a website worth it?

That is the strongest argument for one, not against. A seasonal business has a few weeks to be found by people who have never heard of it, and search is the only channel that works at that speed. It also needs to be ranking before the season starts, which means building it in spring, not in November.

No. The site gets built before there is an invoice, and you decide once you have seen it on your own business.

A five page site is usually ready to look at within a few days of us having your photos and details. What slows it down is nearly always waiting on photos.

Can you promise I will come up first on Google?

No, and nobody honest will. We build the foundation properly — page titles, service and location pages, schema, fast mobile loading, search console setup. Where you land and how quickly is not something anyone can guarantee.
