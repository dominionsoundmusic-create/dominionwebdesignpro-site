Atlanta has more Black-owned businesses than any metro in America, and almost all of them are one person

LendingTree has now named Atlanta the nation's leading metro for Black-owned businesses four years running. In the most recent Census data, 10.7 percent of all employer businesses in the Atlanta metro are Black-owned — the highest share in the country.

The growth behind that number is the part worth sitting with. Metro Atlanta went from 6,795 Black-owned employer firms in 2017 to 13,091 in 2023. That is 6,296 new businesses and 92.7 percent growth in six years.

Now the number almost nobody quotes. According to the Atlanta Wealth Building Initiative, 97 percent of Black-owned businesses in the region are sole proprietorships — one person, no payroll, no employees.

Read those two facts together and you have the whole Atlanta small business web design market in a sentence. The firms with employees get the attention, the press coverage and the agency pitches. They are three percent of the total. The other ninety-seven percent are one person with a trade, a client list and a phone, and most of them are being sold to by nobody.

Why that matters for what a website costs

An agency structured to serve a firm with six employees and a $35,500 average payroll has to price for that client. A sole proprietor cannot carry a $15,000 build and a retainer, so the agency does not call them — and the sole proprietor concludes that a real website is not for people like them. That is a pricing problem being mistaken for a quality problem.

The sectors where this bites hardest

Food is the most visible category of Black-owned business in Atlanta — restaurants, catering, bakeries, food trucks. These are businesses that live or die on being findable at the moment someone is hungry and searching, and many of them are running on a Facebook page and a phone number in a bio link. That is not a small gap. It is the difference between being found and being remembered by whoever already knows you.

What actually gets built

Every site, at every tier:

  • Works on a phone first. Most of the people looking for you are on one, standing somewhere, deciding now.
  • A tap-to-call number in the header. It sounds obvious. A surprising number of small business sites bury the phone number in a footer.
  • A form that reaches you — tested, with the message actually arriving in an inbox you read.
  • Real words about your business, not filler about passion and excellence. Written from a conversation with you.
  • You own it. The domain is yours, the content is yours, and you are not renting your own website back from anyone.

That last one is worth saying plainly. Some builders lease you a site that stops existing the month you stop paying. If you ever want to move, ask before you sign whether you can take it with you.

Before you hire anyone, ours included

Four questions worth asking any web designer in Atlanta:

  • What does it cost, in dollars, before we talk again? A range is a fair answer. A refusal to give one is information.
  • Do I own the site and the domain? If the answer needs a paragraph, the answer is no.
  • What happens when I need a change in six months? Cost, timeline, and whether anyone picks up.
  • Can I see something you built for a business my size? Not their biggest client. One like you.

Those questions are not a sales trap. Ask them here too.
