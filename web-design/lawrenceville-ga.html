In Lawrenceville, your customers are not all searching in the same language

Gwinnett County is one of the most ethnically diverse counties in the Southeast. It crossed one million residents in 2025, up from roughly 166,000 in 1980, and the growth brought large Korean, Vietnamese, Chinese, Indian, Latino and African American communities with it. Lilburn has been named the most diverse place to live in Georgia. Pleasant Hill Road in Duluth is an international business corridor. Norcross has a substantial cluster of Latino-owned construction firms.

Lawrenceville sits in the middle of all of it, as the county seat and the second-oldest city in metro Atlanta.

For a business owner here, that has a consequence most web design advice ignores entirely. Your customer base may search in English, or may not. A restaurant on Highway 120 might get found by someone typing in English on Monday and someone typing in Korean on Tuesday. A contractor in Norcross may do most of his word-of-mouth business in Spanish and most of his online business in English.

Almost nobody building small business websites accounts for that, because most of the advice is written for places where it does not come up.

What that means practically

It is rarely a full second version of your site. More often it is a few decisions: which page gets a second language, whether your business name appears in both scripts so people can find you either way, and whether the phone number is reachable in one tap from every page. Small choices, made on purpose, rather than an expensive translation project.

The courthouse economy

Lawrenceville is the county seat, and the Gwinnett Justice and Administration Center sits at the center of the city. A courthouse generates its own economy — attorneys, paralegals, bail bonds, title companies, process servers, accountants, translators.

Those are businesses where a website does a specific job. Someone looking for a family law attorney in Lawrenceville is not browsing. They are in a difficult week, they are searching on a phone, and they will call one of the first people whose site loads fast and looks like a real practice.

That is a different requirement from a retail site. It needs to load quickly, say plainly what you do and who you serve, make the phone number impossible to miss, and look like an organization that will still exist next month. Nothing clever. Just present, fast and credible at the moment somebody needs you.

Questions worth asking before you hire anyone

  • What does it cost? A range is a fair answer. A refusal to give one tells you something.
  • Do I own the site and the domain? If that needs explaining, the answer is no.
  • What happens when I need a change? What it costs, how long, and whether anyone answers.
  • Can I see one you built for a business my size? Not their biggest client — one like yours.
